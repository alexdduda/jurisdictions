Fiscal federalism, simulated

Who should decide?

Programming languages settled long ago which scope wins: the innermost one. Federations still argue about it. This project writes the argument down twice: as a programming language, and as a simulation that searches for the split of power that leaves people best off.

Findings

What the model and the data say

  1. Canadians disagree with each other far more than provinces do. Between-province disagreement is 8% of within-province disagreement on education, about 20% on the environment and redistribution, and 24% on the carbon tax. The classic case for provincial control, that provinces want different things, is weaker in Canada than usually assumed.
  2. Where provinces do differ, costs explain it. Provinces that emit more per person support the carbon tax less (correlation -0.82 across the ten provinces, p = 0.003).
  3. For climate, the instrument matters more than the level. A carbon price keeps 99% of the attainable gain; one national quantity standard keeps 44%, and a quantity floor provinces may exceed 49%. A national price floor that provinces may exceed, the 2018 design, does as well as a uniform price.
  4. A carbon price is also the rule politics kills. Households see a charge about 19 times the real gain and, surveys found, little of the rebate. Starting from today's arrangement on the ten real provinces, with loss aversion, repeal at elections and the other political parts added, Ottawa keeps today's industrial floor in every sampled world. The visible charge alone does it: take it away and a price floor passes in 77% of worlds. That explains why Canada's consumer charge was unpopular, not when it ended: it survived the 2019 and 2021 elections before the governing party set it to zero in 2025. Across 13 carbon-price votes and elections, prices ended through parties, not directly through voters: when a party pledged to repeal won office, or when the governing party gave the price up. Tested before running on Australia's 2012-2014 price, a case it wasn't built on, the layer correctly predicted the repeal and the national limit on emitters that followed.
  5. Redistribution belongs at the national level in 98% of sampled worlds, even with nobody moving, and for any weight on inequality up to 1.5 (estimates center on 1.26). It is already national, and politics keeps it there in 92% of worlds.
  6. Neither Ottawa nor the provinces raise a dollar more cheaply, and that one number decides schools and the environment. On one basis (2025 income tax, income moving between provinces counted as no loss to Canada) the provincial cost is 1.01 to 1.02 times the federal for Canada as a whole, 0.68 to 1.36 province by province. That leaves the environment a near-tie: a national standard wins 55% of sampled worlds, but a 20% federal matching grant loses least across them and keeps 39% of the gain.
  7. Equalization is the best fixed rule for schools and transit, but not Canada's version. The version that does best takes from richer provinces as well as topping up poorer ones (12% of the gain for schools, against 9% for one-way equalization like Canada's). Neither is a clear winner, and taking money from provinces would need a constitutional amendment.
  8. Health, schools and transit have no clear winner. A national health system wins 60% of sampled worlds, but where it loses it loses badly, so the rule that loses least is a 10% matching grant keeping 3% of the gain. Transit's best rule keeps 4%: US transit agencies' costs rise slightly with size rather than fall, so one national network would not save money.
  9. A legislature of regional delegates is the worst rule tested. Each delegate wants spending in their region paid for by everyone (Besley and Coate's common pool), so it overspends by far more than any gain from coordinating.
  10. Politics costs little where there is little to gain. Outside climate, the political layer keeps today's arrangement in 13% to 23% of worlds and gives up 7% to 36% of a gain that was small to start with. Fiscal crises cost the most, because they cut Ottawa's grants after provinces have planned around them. Measured attachment to the province and the constitution's limits change little.
  11. The verdicts would have been the same decades ago. Rebuilt from only the data available before 1996, 2006 and 2016, every area picks the same rule as today except health with the oldest data, a near-tie either way. Two of three reforms since then moved the model's way; the one that didn't (the 1996 welfare block grant) followed a budget crisis, and was followed by lower and more uneven provincial spending, as the model predicts of provincial choice.
  12. The model does not predict other federations well. On the United States one of six verdicts matches how powers are divided (redistribution), on Australia two (redistribution and health). It recommends a national carbon price for both, which neither has.
  13. Canadians move for income, not services. Within a province, years of rising income bring migrants (+0.32); rising service spending does not (-0.22). Richer provinces spend more on schools, the environment and social protection.

Every factual claim is logged with its sources in the repository's VERIFICATION.md; three calibration errors caught by these checks are listed in § 7, and a bug caught before publishing is described in the repository's validation notes.

§ 1

Scope is jurisdiction

A variable in code and a rule in politics raise the same question: who can set it, and whose setting wins? In most languages the answer is fixed. A function's local variable shadows the global one, and that's the end of it. In Canada it depends on the subject. Ottawa preempts on criminal law. It sets a floor on the cannabis age (18) that Quebec raised to 21. And from 1966 to 1996 it paid half of provinces' social assistance costs while they ran the programs (the Canada Assistance Plan).

scopelang makes each of those a rule a variable can carry. A write that breaks the rule is struck down: reported, not applied, and the program carries on, the way a court voids one law without stopping the government.

scope canada {
  law cannabis_age: floor = 18

  scope quebec  { cannabis_age = 21 }
  scope quebec  {
    scope montreal { cannabis_age = 18 }
  }
}

§ 2

A model of the trade-off

The simulation is a grid of 16 regions and 1,600 people. Each person has an income and a taste for each public service. Every round, each region's government does what its median resident wants, within the national rules. Then people look around and move if another region suits them better and the move is worth its cost.

For each policy area, a search picks one of the rules above and its level, plus two more grant designs and a legislature: equalization that also takes from richer regions, a foundation grant (Ottawa funds a minimum, regions top up, like Australia's school funding), and a national level set by a legislature of regional delegates (Besley and Coate's common pool). It's balancing forces that pull in opposite directions:

Pushes power down to regionsPushes power up to the nation
Regions want different things, so one national level fits nobody exactlyBenefits leak to neighbours (clean air, rivers), which a region ignores, so it under-provides
People can move to the region that suits themOne national network can be cheaper than sixteen
Money run through the national budget has overheadMobile high earners can escape regional redistribution, which shrinks local tax bases

Health, added later, works like schools. A sixth area, climate, works differently: cutting emissions is paid for by emitters rather than budgets, and costs differ widely between provinces. There the choice is between letting provinces decide, one national quantity standard, and a carbon price, either uniform or as a national floor provinces can exceed.

These are the ingredients of Wallace Oates' decentralisation theorem and the standard objections to it. The tests check that the model reproduces the textbook results. Left alone, a region provides only taste × (1 − spillover), and a matching grant at the spillover rate restores the best level.

§ 3

Calibrated to Canada

An automated review of the first version made the obvious objection: with every number guessed, the verdicts mostly restate the guesses. So wherever Canadian evidence exists, the guess is replaced by a measurement. Spending by function in every province (Statistics Canada, 2015 to 2024), interprovincial migration, income inequality, published estimates of what it costs to raise a dollar federally and provincially, and 37,000 respondents to the 2019 Canadian Election Study.

Disagreement between provinces is the hard one to measure, because what a province spends mixes what its people want with what things cost and what it can afford. It is measured five ways: spending per person, spending as a share of the budget, spending after removing the effect of provincial income, education spending per school-age child, and what people say in the survey. They agree.

§ 4

The answer, across possible worlds per policy area

Even calibrated, some numbers are only known as a range, and some (how much benefit spills across borders, how much one national system saves) have no Canadian data at all. So the model is solved in worlds per policy area, sampled evenly across what remains uncertain, with every candidate policy scored in every world on the same random seeds. Each row also shows what the same method concluded when every number was a wide guess.

Committing to one policy without knowing the true numbers is also what a real government does. So the recommendation for each area answers a harder question than "what wins most often": which single rule, at one fixed level, loses least across the whole range? Levels are on the model's own scale, where 0.5 is what an average resident would choose if their region kept every benefit for itself.

§ 5

Which assumptions decide it

For each parameter, the bar shows how much the winning rule changes between the low half of its range and the high half (0 means no change, 100 means a different winner every time). These are the numbers worth arguing about. The rest barely matter.

§ 6

Where the answer flips

Some numbers no Canadian source measures: how much of a service's benefit spills into other provinces, and how much one national system saves. A verdict that depends on them is only as good as the guess. So instead of a verdict, these maps show the winning rule across those numbers, with everything that is measured held at its Canadian value. If you know the real number, read off the answer.

Cities

One province, many cities

Transit's city level used to be one city and one countryside per province. Now every census metropolitan area is in the model: all 35 from the 2016 Census, with Ottawa-Gatineau split between Ontario and Quebec. Each modelled region contains its province's metropolitan areas in proportion to their population, and how much each one wants transit is read from how much its commuters already use it. Small areas are pooled with others of similar transit use, which leaves 25 distinct city places and a countryside in each region.

2016 is deliberate: it is the last census before the pandemic changed commuting. The 2021 Census was taken mid-lockdown.

§ 7

Does the model reproduce Canada?

Calibration fits the model to Canadian inputs. Validation asks whether it then reproduces Canadian outcomes it was never fitted to. The calibrated model was run under Canada's arrangement (provinces run schools, the environment and transit; redistribution is a national floor provinces can top up), and its simulated regions were put through the same statistical tests as the ten provinces.

Corrections. Three errors in earlier versions of this page were found and fixed:

  • How much provinces disagree was overstated by a factor of two. Provincial spending measures what a service costs, and in the model cost grows with the square of the service level, so spending varies twice as much as the level does.
  • The calibration of regional productivity, done in a model with all four policy areas, didn't carry over to the single-area worlds the analysis uses: almost none of them matched Canada's regional income gaps. They now have their own calibration.
  • The cost of raising money was sampled over ranges that excluded the study's own cited sources (found by the automated review below). The federal range was 20% to 100% extra per dollar against a cited 102% to 186%, and the provincial range was tied to the federal one. Both now span the published estimates. A first fix sampled the two over ranges of different widths, which quietly made provincial money 40% dearer on average; the provincial-to-federal ratio is now symmetric, so neither level is cheaper in expectation.
§ 8

Checks on the method

Ahead

Looking ahead: dated verdicts and what to watch

The model describes Canada's long-run structure; it is not a forecast. Two of its measured inputs have official or trend projections, so those verdicts can be dated. The rest turn on numbers nobody has measured, so the useful forward-looking output is a watch list: the value at which each verdict flips, and how close the best estimate is.

Dated verdicts
Verdicts with uncertainty that grows with the horizon

Measured inputs drift: across the backtest's decades, provincial spending disagreement moved up to 12% to 27% a decade, inequality 8%, the spread of emissions per person 22%. For a verdict dated h years ahead, each measured range is widened by that drift times h/10 and the analysis rerun on the same sampled worlds. No verdict moves: every area picks the same rule in 2036 and 2046 as today, and the share of the attainable gain kept moves by at most 6 points (redistribution, 87% to 81%). The carbon price still wins in 98.5% of 2046 worlds; a national standard takes the other 1.5%.

Watch list

Backtest

What the model would have said at the time

The model says what should be decided where, so it can't be proven right because governments did something, or wrong because they didn't. But it can be asked two fair questions. With only the data that existed before each reform, would it have reached the verdict it reaches today? And does that verdict point the way Canada moved? Every measured input (spending by function, inequality, migration, regional incomes, fiscal capacity, emissions) was rebuilt from the ten years before 1996, 2006 and 2016, and the whole analysis rerun on the same sampled worlds and seeds.

Three reforms, judged with only the data of the time
Every verdict, then and now

Stable where it matters. With data from 1986-1995, 1996-2005 or 2006-2015, schools, the environment, transit and redistribution pick the same rule as with today's data (one level moves a notch: national redistribution at 0.3 rather than 0.25 with 1996-2005 data). The share of the attainable gain each keeps moves by at most 11 points (the environment, 29% with the oldest data against 39% now). Health is the one change: with the oldest data it picks equalization both ways instead of a small matching grant, both keeping under 7% of the gain. Disagreement between provinces, inequality and migration all changed over those decades (migration fell from 1.09% to 0.79% a year), but not by enough to move a verdict.

Two of three reforms point the model's way. Ontario moving Toronto-area transit from cities to a provincial body moves away from the model's weaker option (cities deciding alone does worse than provinces deciding), and the national carbon price floor moves toward what the model recommends with the data of the time. The 1996 block grant moved the other way. It came out of a budget crisis: the 1995 budget cut transfers by about 15% (40% of projected cash), after a 1990 cap on matching payments to the richest provinces that the Supreme Court upheld in 1991. The political layer below adds such crises.

Where it disagreed, the later data look like what the model predicts of provincial choice. After Ottawa stopped matching welfare spending, provincial and local social-services spending fell from 4.4% to 3.4% of provincial GDP (fiscal years 1988-95 against 1997-2008), and the gap between provinces widened (coefficient of variation of spending per person 0.28 to 0.35). The late-1990s recovery also cut welfare caseloads and the 1995 federal budget cut transfers, so this is consistent with the model, not proof of it.

What it missed. The welfare model recommended a carbon price in 2016 and still does, but the consumer charge was set to zero in 2025 and repealed in law in 2026. The "who loses" analysis below finds no province that loses in real welfare, so the repeal reflects something the welfare model leaves out. The political layer below finds it: how visible the charge is against the rebate.

Opinion over time: how far apart provinces are, in units of disagreement within one

Canadian Election Studies 1993 to 2019, the same method as the 2019 calibration (sampling noise removed). Same question where it exists: should government spend more, less or the same on education and the environment (2004 on), and how much should be done to reduce the gap between rich and poor (2004 on). Marked * where an older study only asked a related question: cutting education spending (1993, 1997), environment against jobs (1993 to 2011), the government doing more on the income gap (1993, 1997). Climate in 2008: the Liberal Green Shift carbon tax "would really hurt the economy", reversed. Blank where not asked; 0 where the gap is within sampling noise.

The opinion data were the one input the backtest couldn't date; now they are, and nothing moves. On the same questions, provinces' gap on redistribution held at 0.19 to 0.26 of the disagreement inside a province from 2004 to 2019, and on the environment 0.12 to 0.20. Schools swing more (0.06 to 0.25) and the provinces' order isn't stable from one study to the next, which fits schools being the area with no clear winner. The 2008 carbon-tax question puts provinces at 0.18 against 2019's 0.24, in nearly the same order (correlation of provincial averages 0.90).

Only two inputs depend on these numbers. With 2004's figure for schools, disagreement inside a province can fall from the model's 0.25-0.30 to about 0.10; with 2008's for climate, the gap between provinces narrows slightly. Both widened ranges are now the default, so every verdict on this page already allows for them: schools still have no clear winner and climate still picks a carbon price (99%). The environment is unaffected: even its largest dated gap leaves disagreement inside provinces above the model's ceiling.

Abroad

The same model on the United States and Australia

Three Canadian reforms are a small sample. A stronger test is another federation: give the model that country's measurements and see whether its verdicts match how the country divides powers. For each country every measured input was rebuilt from its own statistics, and the regions are its real states, sized by population and joined where they share a border. United States: state and local spending by function (Census Bureau), market-income inequality (OECD), state income (BEA), migration between states (Current Population Survey), CO2 per person (EIA). Australia: state and local spending by purpose (ABS Government Finance Statistics), interstate departures and household income (ABS), inequality (OECD), state emissions inventories (DCCEEW). Mobility was refitted to each country's migration and spread of incomes. What can't be measured keeps its Canadian range.

The model does not predict how other federations divide powers. On the United States one of six verdicts matches clearly (national redistribution) and one partly (schools: the model says equalization; Title I grants aim at poorer districts but are a small share of school money). On Australia two match (national redistribution and matching grants for health, which the Commonwealth pays 45% of the growth in) and one partly (schools: a small national floor, where Australia funds a share of a needs-based standard). Both get a national carbon price, which neither has; Australia had one from 2012 to 2014.

The fit to each country's mobility is weak. Only 0% to 27% of the sampled US worlds, and 3% to 48% of the Australian ones, reproduce that country's migration and income spread, against about 60% for Canada. The mobility settings were refitted, but the model's movers don't match both targets at once on fifty or six states. Treat these runs as a test the model mostly fails, not as advice to either country.

Where it fails, it fails the same way as in Canada: it recommends a carbon price and more national money than federations choose. The political layer above explains the carbon price in Canada; it has not been built for the United States or Australia.

Losers

Who loses under each rule

A rule can win on totals and still not last if its losses fall on a few places. For each recommended rule, 40 sampled worlds (3 seeds each) were run under the rule and under provinces deciding, and the change in welfare measured for the people who started in each of the 16 regions.

With real provinces: each region's own measured tastes, emissions and income

Each cell: the group's typical change as a multiple of the average gain (1.0 = gains like the average Canadian), and in brackets the share of sampled worlds in which it is worse off. Regions follow the model's 16: 4 Quebec, 6 Ontario, 2 British Columbia, 2 Alberta, 1 Manitoba and Saskatchewan, 1 Atlantic. Grouping hides Saskatchewan inside Manitoba.

Named, the losers are mostly Alberta and Saskatchewan. With every region set to its real province, the environment's 20% matching grant leaves Alberta worse off in 81% of worlds and Saskatchewan in 74%; Quebec never loses. Alberta also loses in about half the worlds under the best rules for transit and health. On real welfare a carbon price leaves every province better off in almost every world, Saskatchewan least (about two fifths of the average gain).

National redistribution has the clearest losers: the provinces that pay in. Ontario and Alberta are worse off in about three quarters of worlds, while the Atlantic provinces gain four to seven times the average. That matches the politics: in 2021 Albertans voted 61.7% to remove equalization from the Constitution (a non-binding referendum).

The verdicts barely change. Giving regions their real provinces' values instead of random ones keeps the same rule for schools, the environment, health and climate, and national redistribution one notch higher (0.3). Transit switches to a small national floor, a near-tie with no clear winner either way.

Equalization both ways has scattered losers. For schools, about a fifth of provinces are worse off in a typical world (a third of the 16 model regions), with Saskatchewan worse off in 40% of worlds; the Atlantic provinces gain about twice the average. Transit's best rule gains almost nothing on average and leaves Alberta worse off in about half the worlds. With no clear winner and those losers, there is no case for moving transit from the provinces.

Politics

Which rules would pass a vote

The model scores rules by real welfare; voters judge them by what they see. Two additions, applied after each simulation: under a carbon price households see the charge on every tonne they still emit but, as surveys found in Canada, little of the rebate; and a rule must win the regions holding a majority of people (or two thirds, standing in for the amending formula). Costs of quantity standards are buried in prices, so only a price has a visible payment.

A carbon price passes in every sampled world on real welfare, and in none once payments are visible. The charge households pay is about 19 times the real gain, so most regions would see a net gain only if voters noticed about 94% of the rebate (87% to 98% across worlds). With the ten real provinces the charge is 15 times the gain and the rebate share needed is 84% (42% to 95%); the price still passes in only 3% of worlds. Surveys put awareness of the rebate at 55% to 75%, and those aware underestimated it. The best rule that does pass is a quantity floor, which keeps about half the gain (80% with the real provinces). Canada's 2025 change, ending the consumer charge but keeping industrial pricing and regulation, is the model's best passable rule.

Elsewhere politics costs little where the case is clear. National redistribution and the environment's best rules pass in most worlds. Schools, transit and health, with no clear winner, are where votes bind: under a two-thirds rule nothing passes in 8% of worlds for schools, 16% for transit and 21% for health. With the real provinces, national redistribution is the exception: Ontario and Alberta, together just over half the people, oppose it in most worlds, it passes a majority in only 34%, and nothing passes two thirds in about half the worlds. Since it is already national, that is a reason it is hard to undo rather than to do.

Realities

Adding the politics the model left out

The welfare model says what a planner who cares only about welfare would pick. Canada's arrangements differ from it in ways the model can name. So a political layer was added on top, on the ten real provinces, starting from what Canada does today. It has seven parts. Each is measured where possible; the rest are sampled over a range and labelled.

  • Status quo. A reform is judged against today's arrangement, not against provinces deciding alone.
  • Loss aversion. A loss weighs 1.8 to 2.1 times an equal gain: the 95% interval of the mean of 607 estimates (Brown, Imai, Vieider and Camerer, 2024).
  • Visible payments. People see the carbon charge but not all of the rebate. Awareness of the rebate was 55% in Ontario and 75% in Saskatchewan, and those who knew underestimated it. The share of the rebate voters notice is sampled from 0 to 60%.
  • Attachment to the province. Some people want their province, not Ottawa, to decide whatever the service. Each province's level is measured from three Canadian Election Study questions (1997 to 2011): federal or provincial power, whether Ottawa treats the province fairly, and which government looks after you. How much each kind of rule puts Ottawa in charge is an assumption. How much attachment weighs against services isn't measured: it is sampled from nothing up to the area's whole gain.
  • The constitution and the courts. Ottawa can act alone through its own powers, its spending power and what the courts have upheld. That needs a Commons majority, taken here as provinces holding more than half the people. A mandate in a provincial field needs the amending formula: seven provinces with half the people.
  • Repeal. A carbon price passed by Parliament must keep a majority through the next three elections, each with a fresh draw of how much of the rebate voters notice. Canada held nine general elections from 2000 to 2025. A constitutional change can't be repealed by an election.
  • Fiscal crises. In a budget crisis Ottawa cuts its own money: grants and federal programmes fall 15% to 40%, the range of the 1995 budget's cut to provincial transfers. Mandates, standards and prices cost Ottawa nothing and are untouched. The Supreme Court confirmed in 1991 that Ottawa may cut its own grants unilaterally. The chance of a crisis in a decade, 30% to 60%, comes from the confirmed episodes since 1990.
Attachment to the province (net share siding with the province; Canadian Election Studies 1997 to 2011)
What Ottawa would do, starting from today

Each area: 40 sampled worlds, 3 seeds each, ten real provinces. Planner: the rule with the largest real gain over today's arrangement, if any. With politics: the rule Ottawa can pass with the largest expected gain as voters see it, discounted for repeal and fiscal crises; otherwise today's arrangement stays.

Which part of the politics does the work: share of worlds in which today's arrangement stays

Left: everything except one part. Right: that part alone (with the status quo). Repeal only acts through visible payments, so on its own it changes nothing.

What Ottawa can do alone

Climate: politics reverses the verdict, and visible payments alone explain it. A planner would replace today's arrangement with a carbon price in 88% of worlds. With the political parts added, today's arrangement stays in all of them: the uniform price would need a constitutional amendment, and the price floor Ottawa can pass alone loses the vote as voters see it. Switch off only the visible payments and a price floor passes in 77% of worlds; switch on only the visible payments and today's arrangement stays everywhere. Loss aversion alone keeps today's arrangement in 23% of worlds, against 12% for the planner. This explains why the consumer charge was unpopular. It doesn't explain when it ended: the charge survived the 2019 and 2021 elections (see the table below).

Tested on a case it wasn't built on: Australia's carbon price, July 2012 to July 2014. The layer was built after Canada's repeal, so explaining that repeal proves little. Rerun on Australia's six states with Australian data and Australia's rules (the Commonwealth could enact any of these rules alone), two predictions were committed before the first run: a price adopted from no national policy would be repealed at the first election, and with politics the rule chosen would be a national quantity standard. Both held. A price survives one election in 0.5% of sampled worlds. With politics the Commonwealth picks a national quantity standard in 78% of worlds and a price in none, where a planner would pick a price in 76%. Australia has limited large emitters' emissions under the Safeguard Mechanism since 2016.

The first prediction is weak evidence, and points to what the layer misses. In Australia, as in Canada, a price keeps a majority only if voters notice 85% to 100% of the compensation, so the layer predicts the repeal of any visible carbon price. That is wrong for British Columbia's carbon tax (July 2008 to April 2025) and Sweden's (since 1991). Whatever let those last is not in the model. The second prediction is the real test, and it differs from Canada for a reason the layer names: in Canada the model treats a uniform national standard as needing a constitutional amendment (an interpretation of the 2021 ruling), while in Australia it doesn't.

Two patterns, and the layer gets one. Direct votes on a new price went against it all three times, as the layer predicts. But the one direct vote on repealing a price that already existed kept it by 62% to 38%, which the layer gets wrong. Elections aren't referendums: in the five where a major party pledged repeal, the price ended exactly when that party won (three times) and survived when it lost (twice). Carbon prices end through parties: a pledge that wins office, or a governing party that gives up. The layer has no parties, which is why it can say a price is unpopular but not when it ends. A model of party competition would fix that. With 13 events it would be fitted to these cases rather than tested on them, so it isn't built yet.

Redistribution: already where the model wants it. Today's national arrangement is the planner's own choice in 92% of worlds, and politics changes nothing. Handing it to the provinces would pass a Commons vote in 78% of worlds, with Ontario and Alberta for it, but voters elsewhere lose more than they gain. A fiscal crisis is the one part that moves it: on its own it lowers the share of worlds keeping today's arrangement to 69%.

Schools, the environment, transit and health: small costs. Politics keeps today's arrangement in 23%, 17%, 22% and 13% of worlds, against 15%, 17%, 16% and 13% for the planner, and gives up 25%, 7%, 8% and 36% of the planner's (small) gain over today. Fiscal crises cost the most: without them, the loss for schools and health nearly vanishes, because a crisis cuts Ottawa's money after provinces have planned around it, so national programmes and grants are worth less in expectation. Loss aversion is the next largest. A national school standard would need an amendment; it passes the amending formula in 30% of worlds.

Attachment and the constitution matter less than expected. Measured attachment differs only modestly between provinces (from -0.04 in Ontario to 0.34 in Newfoundland and Labrador and 0.31 in Quebec on a scale from -1 to 1), and the rules that win mostly run through Ottawa's spending power, which it can use alone. Switching attachment off changes the choice in at most 4% of worlds per area. How much attachment should weigh is not measured, so this is a finding about the measured differences between provinces, not about identity in general.

What this layer still leaves out: parties, credit-claiming, lobbying, courts' future rulings, Quebec's distinct position beyond what the surveys measure, and how attachment would respond to a reform itself.

No published study was found that scores Canada's division of powers across several policy areas with one calibrated model. The closest in spirit is Alesina and Spolaore's The Size of Nations (2003), the same trade-off between scale and differing preferences applied to borders rather than to who decides.

§ 9

What the model can't tell you

Sampling ranges fixes uncertainty about the numbers. It can't fix the model's structure. These would each move the answer, and none of them is in the simulation:

  • Data age. The data describe long-run structure (city sizes, the urban-rural split, which provinces emit more) from 2016 to 2024, and survey opinion from 2019. Structure changes slowly, and a reform to who decides would last decades, so a pre-pandemic year is arguably a better guide than 2021. Opinion and prices move faster: carbon-tax support has likely shifted since 2019. The model is not a forecast; where a verdict hinges on a fast-moving number, the threshold maps in § 6 show what value would flip it.
  • Politics. In the welfare model governments are honest median voters. The political layer ("Adding the politics the model left out") adds the status quo, loss aversion, visible payments, attachment to the province, the constitution, repeal and fiscal crises, but not parties, capture by industry or lobbying.
  • Learning. Regions can't copy each other's successful experiments. That's one of the strongest real arguments for decentralisation, so the model probably understates it.
  • Uniformity. National policy has to be the same everywhere. Real national programs tailor somewhat, which narrows the case for regional control.
  • Who pays. Poorer regions now find local money dearer, and equalization is one of the rules tested. But equalization here tops up one service at a time; Canada's is unconditional and covers everything at once.
  • Scale. The main analysis uses sixteen regions on a grid. Rerun on the ten real provinces, sized by population and joined where they border, it picks the same rules except a near-tie for transit ("Who loses under each rule").

What two automated reviews challenged

The study's claims were run twice through ai-auditor, which extracts assumptions, searches for counter-evidence and proposes alternative answers. It is one model reviewing another, so its points are challenges, not verified findings, and studies it cites are checked against their sources before this page uses them. The second review gave the recommendations a confidence of 0.15 out of 1.

Fixed:

  • The verdicts restated guessed ranges. Replaced by Canadian measurements wherever any exist (§ 3); where none exist, verdicts are shown as thresholds (§ 6).
  • The cost-of-funds ranges excluded the cited evidence. A real error; corrected and re-run (see Corrections in § 7).
  • Equalization was never tested, though it is how Canada actually handles provinces' different ability to pay. Now one of the rules, with a version that also takes from richer regions.
  • The welfare measure was untested. Redistribution is now mapped across a range of weights on inequality (§ 6).
  • Overclaims. The page called the services-migration test a match (it isn't), presented an argument about the redistribution failure as if shown, and gave schools a verdict that is really a close call. All three are corrected.

Still open:

  • Transit and cities: addressed. Every census metropolitan area is now in the model ("One province, many cities"), and "cities decide" is a rule. Cities win only when almost no commuting crosses the metro boundary, and national wins only if one national system saves about 10% or more, which published cost studies suggest it wouldn't. Still missing: a Canadian estimate of how transit costs change with network size, and commuting between neighbouring metro areas (Toronto, Hamilton and Oshawa are separate areas in the census but share a commuter rail network).
  • Carbon pricing: now modelled as a separate climate area with prices, price floors and provincial differences in abatement costs (§ 4). Canada set its consumer carbon charge to zero on April 1, 2025; industrial pricing for large emitters continues under a national benchmark. Still missing: revenue use, trade exposure, and emissions leaking abroad.
  • Schools and language: partly addressed. Regions now have Canada's official-language profile, and a national standard costs minority-language residents unless it accommodates them (§ 6). Education is provincial under section 93 of the Constitution Act, 1867, which also protects denominational schools in some provinces (Quebec removed its guarantee in 1997); section 23 of the Charter protects minority-language schooling. A welfare total can't weigh those protections, which is the point of having them.
  • Provinces want different amounts of redistribution, and Quebec's residents move least: 0.35% a year leave for another province, against 0.55% to 1.99% elsewhere. Together these could favour a national floor with provincial top-ups.
  • Constitutional jurisdiction and Quebec's place in the federation are not welfare questions. A simulation can inform one consideration among several; it can't settle what Canada should do.

Read the results as a map of which features of a policy area push it up or down a level. They are not a verdict on any particular country.

§ 10

Run it yourself

Plain Python, standard library only. The optimised arrangement is saved as a scopelang program, so you can edit a law and re-score it.

python3 -m scopelang examples/min_wage.jur --tree
python3 -m tiebout study          # one hand-picked world, phase maps
python3 -m tiebout uncertainty    # sampled ranges, robust policies
python3 -m tiebout evaluate results/robust_arrangement.jur
