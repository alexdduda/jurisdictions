Fiscal federalism, simulated

Who should decide?

Programming languages settled long ago which scope wins: the innermost one. Federations still argue about it. This project writes the argument down twice: as a programming language, and as a simulation that searches for the split of power that leaves people best off.

Findings

What the model and the data say

  1. Canadians disagree with each other far more than provinces do. Between-province disagreement is 8% of within-province disagreement on education, about 20% on the environment and redistribution, and 24% on the carbon tax. The classic case for provincial control, that provinces want different things, is weaker in Canada than usually assumed.
  2. Where provinces do differ, costs explain it. Provinces that emit more per person support the carbon tax less (correlation -0.82 across the ten provinces, p = 0.003).
  3. For climate, the instrument matters more than the level. A carbon price keeps 99% of the attainable gain; one national quantity standard keeps 44%. A national price floor that provinces may exceed, the 2018 design, does as well as a uniform price.
  4. Neither Ottawa nor the provinces raise a dollar more cheaply. Published estimates seemed to settle schools and the environment either way, but they mixed years, methods and the provinces' own point of view. On one basis (2025 income tax, income moving between provinces counted as no loss to Canada) the provincial cost is 1.01 to 1.02 times the federal for Canada as a whole, 0.68 to 1.36 province by province. That makes the environment narrowly national and keeps schools provincial, with equalization.
  5. Redistribution belongs at the national level in 98% of sampled worlds, even with nobody moving, and for any weight on inequality up to 1.5 (estimates center on 1.26).
  6. Transit stays with the provinces and their cities. A national network would win only if it saved about 10% or more over separate systems. Cost studies find savings from size only for small operators (in Italy, below the sector's average output; for large US systems, little or none), so merging Canada's already-large metro systems would save little. With savings sampled at 0% to 10%, no fixed national rule beats provincial control; cities win only where almost no commuting crosses the metro boundary.
  7. Equalization, Canada's actual tool, beats centralizing where provinces raise money cheaply: it closes poorer provinces' gap in ability to pay without taking decisions away from them, and wins about a quarter of the sampled worlds for schools.
  8. A carbon price is the best climate rule and the hardest to pass. Households see a charge about 19 times the real gain and, surveys found, little of the rebate; it would take voters noticing about 94% of the rebate for most regions to feel better off. The best rule that passes, a quantity floor, keeps about half the gain, and is roughly what Canada kept in 2025.
  9. The verdicts would have been the same decades ago. Rebuilt from only the data available before 1996, 2006 and 2016, and with opinion from election studies back to 1993, the model picks today's rule in every area with a clear winner. Two of three reforms since then moved its way; the one that didn't (the 1996 welfare block grant) was followed by lower and more uneven provincial spending, as the model predicts of provincial choice.
  10. On the United States, two of five verdicts match. Rebuilt from US data, the model says national for redistribution and the environment, as the US largely does. It says a national carbon price, which the US lacks, and equalization for schools and transit, where state spending tracks state income far more steeply than in Canada.
  11. Canadians move for income, not services. Within a province, years of rising income bring migrants (+0.32); rising service spending does not (-0.22). Richer provinces spend more on schools, the environment and social protection.

Every factual claim is logged with its sources in the repository's VERIFICATION.md; three calibration errors caught by these checks are listed in § 7, and a bug caught before publishing is described in the repository's validation notes.

§ 1

Scope is jurisdiction

A variable in code and a rule in politics raise the same question: who can set it, and whose setting wins? In most languages the answer is fixed. A function's local variable shadows the global one, and that's the end of it. In Canada it depends on the subject. Ottawa preempts on criminal law. It sets a floor on the cannabis age (18) that Quebec raised to 21. And from 1966 to 1996 it paid half of provinces' social assistance costs while they ran the programs (the Canada Assistance Plan).

scopelang makes each of those a rule a variable can carry. A write that breaks the rule is struck down: reported, not applied, and the program carries on, the way a court voids one law without stopping the government.

scope canada {
  law cannabis_age: floor = 18

  scope quebec  { cannabis_age = 21 }
  scope quebec  {
    scope montreal { cannabis_age = 18 }
  }
}

§ 2

A model of the trade-off

The simulation is a grid of 16 regions and 1,600 people. Each person has an income and a taste for each public service. Every round, each region's government does what its median resident wants, within the national rules. Then people look around and move if another region suits them better and the move is worth its cost.

For each policy area, a search picks one of the five rules above and its level. It's balancing forces that pull in opposite directions:

Pushes power down to regionsPushes power up to the nation
Regions want different things, so one national level fits nobody exactlyBenefits leak to neighbours (clean air, rivers), which a region ignores, so it under-provides
People can move to the region that suits themOne national network can be cheaper than sixteen
Money run through the national budget has overheadMobile high earners can escape regional redistribution, which shrinks local tax bases

A fifth area, climate, works differently: cutting emissions is paid for by emitters rather than budgets, and costs differ widely between provinces. There the choice is between letting provinces decide, one national quantity standard, and a carbon price, either uniform or as a national floor provinces can exceed.

These are the ingredients of Wallace Oates' decentralisation theorem and the standard objections to it. The tests check that the model reproduces the textbook results. Left alone, a region provides only taste × (1 − spillover), and a matching grant at the spillover rate restores the best level.

§ 3

Calibrated to Canada

An automated review of the first version made the obvious objection: with every number guessed, the verdicts mostly restate the guesses. So wherever Canadian evidence exists, the guess is replaced by a measurement. Spending by function in every province (Statistics Canada, 2015 to 2024), interprovincial migration, income inequality, published estimates of what it costs to raise a dollar federally and provincially, and 37,000 respondents to the 2019 Canadian Election Study.

Disagreement between provinces is the hard one to measure, because what a province spends mixes what its people want with what things cost and what it can afford. It is measured five ways: spending per person, spending as a share of the budget, spending after removing the effect of provincial income, education spending per school-age child, and what people say in the survey. They agree.

§ 4

The answer, across possible worlds per policy area

Even calibrated, some numbers are only known as a range, and some (how much benefit spills across borders, how much one national system saves) have no Canadian data at all. So the model is solved in worlds per policy area, sampled evenly across what remains uncertain, with every candidate policy scored in every world on the same random seeds. Each row also shows what the same method concluded when every number was a wide guess.

Committing to one policy without knowing the true numbers is also what a real government does. So the recommendation for each area answers a harder question than "what wins most often": which single rule, at one fixed level, loses least across the whole range? Levels are on the model's own scale, where 0.5 is what an average resident would choose if their region kept every benefit for itself.

Which assumptions decide it

For each parameter, the bar shows how much the winning rule changes between the low half of its range and the high half (0 means no change, 100 means a different winner every time). These are the numbers worth arguing about. The rest barely matter.

§ 6

Where the answer flips

Some numbers no Canadian source measures: how much of a service's benefit spills into other provinces, and how much one national system saves. A verdict that depends on them is only as good as the guess. So instead of a verdict, these maps show the winning rule across those numbers, with everything that is measured held at its Canadian value. If you know the real number, read off the answer.

Cities

One province, many cities

Transit's city level used to be one city and one countryside per province. Now every census metropolitan area is in the model: all 35 from the 2016 Census, with Ottawa-Gatineau split between Ontario and Quebec. Each modelled region contains its province's metropolitan areas in proportion to their population, and how much each one wants transit is read from how much its commuters already use it. Small areas are pooled with others of similar transit use, which leaves 25 distinct city places and a countryside in each region.

2016 is deliberate: it is the last census before the pandemic changed commuting. The 2021 Census was taken mid-lockdown.

§ 7

Does the model reproduce Canada?

Calibration fits the model to Canadian inputs. Validation asks whether it then reproduces Canadian outcomes it was never fitted to. The calibrated model was run under Canada's arrangement (provinces run schools, the environment and transit; redistribution is a national floor provinces can top up), and its simulated regions were put through the same statistical tests as the ten provinces.

Corrections. Three errors in earlier versions of this page were found and fixed:

  • How much provinces disagree was overstated by a factor of two. Provincial spending measures what a service costs, and in the model cost grows with the square of the service level, so spending varies twice as much as the level does.
  • The calibration of regional productivity, done in a model with all four policy areas, didn't carry over to the single-area worlds the analysis uses: almost none of them matched Canada's regional income gaps. They now have their own calibration.
  • The cost of raising money was sampled over ranges that excluded the study's own cited sources (found by the automated review below). The federal range was 20% to 100% extra per dollar against a cited 102% to 186%, and the provincial range was tied to the federal one. Both now span the published estimates. A first fix sampled the two over ranges of different widths, which quietly made provincial money 40% dearer on average; the provincial-to-federal ratio is now symmetric, so neither level is cheaper in expectation.
§ 8

Checks on the method

Ahead

Looking ahead: dated verdicts and what to watch

The model describes Canada's long-run structure; it is not a forecast. Two of its measured inputs have official or trend projections, so those verdicts can be dated. The rest turn on numbers nobody has measured, so the useful forward-looking output is a watch list: the value at which each verdict flips, and how close the best estimate is.

Dated verdicts
Verdicts with uncertainty that grows with the horizon

Measured inputs drift: across the backtest's decades, provincial spending disagreement moved up to 12% to 27% a decade, inequality 8%, the spread of emissions per person 22%. For a verdict dated h years ahead, each measured range is widened by that drift times h/10 and the analysis rerun on the same sampled worlds. No verdict moves: every area picks the same rule in 2036 and 2046 as today, and the share of the attainable gain kept moves by at most 6 points (redistribution, 87% to 81%). The carbon price still wins in 98.5% of 2046 worlds; a national standard takes the other 1.5%.

Watch list

Backtest

What the model would have said at the time

The model says what should be decided where, so it can't be proven right because governments did something, or wrong because they didn't. But it can be asked two fair questions. With only the data that existed before each reform, would it have reached the verdict it reaches today? And does that verdict point the way Canada moved? Every measured input (spending by function, inequality, migration, regional incomes, fiscal capacity, emissions) was rebuilt from the ten years before 1996, 2006 and 2016, and the whole analysis rerun on the same sampled worlds and seeds.

Three reforms, judged with only the data of the time
Every verdict, then and now

Stable where it matters. Every verdict with a clear winner (the environment, redistribution, climate) picks the same rule with 1986-1995, 1996-2005 or 2006-2015 data as with today's (one level moves a notch: national redistribution at 0.3 rather than 0.25 with 1996-2005 data), and the share of the attainable gain each keeps moves by at most 3 points. Schools and transit, which have no clear winner, swap between near-ties: with the two earlier decades' data schools pick a small matching grant rather than equalization, and transit a small national floor rather than equalization, each keeping within a point or two of the same small gain. Disagreement between provinces, inequality and migration all changed over those decades (migration fell from 1.09% to 0.79% a year), but not by enough to move a verdict. Restricting to sampled worlds as mobile as each decade gives the same answers.

Two of three reforms point the model's way. Ontario moving Toronto-area transit from cities to a provincial body moves away from the model's weaker option (cities deciding alone does worse than provinces deciding), and the national carbon price floor moves toward what the model recommends with the data of the time. The 1996 block grant moved the other way.

Where it disagreed, the later data look like what the model predicts of provincial choice. After Ottawa stopped matching welfare spending, provincial and local social-services spending fell from 4.4% to 3.4% of provincial GDP (fiscal years 1988-95 against 1997-2008), and the gap between provinces widened (coefficient of variation of spending per person 0.28 to 0.35). The late-1990s recovery also cut welfare caseloads and the 1995 federal budget cut transfers, so this is consistent with the model, not proof of it.

What it missed. The model recommended a carbon price in 2016 and still does, but the consumer charge was set to zero in 2025. The "who loses" analysis below finds no region that loses in the model, so the repeal reflects something it leaves out: how visible the cost was against the rebate, and politics generally.

Opinion over time: how far apart provinces are, in units of disagreement within one

Canadian Election Studies 1993 to 2019, the same method as the 2019 calibration (sampling noise removed). Same question where it exists: should government spend more, less or the same on education and the environment (2004 on), and how much should be done to reduce the gap between rich and poor (2004 on). Marked * where an older study only asked a related question: cutting education spending (1993, 1997), environment against jobs (1993 to 2011), the government doing more on the income gap (1993, 1997). Climate in 2008: the Liberal Green Shift carbon tax "would really hurt the economy", reversed. Blank where not asked; 0 where the gap is within sampling noise.

The opinion data were the one input the backtest couldn't date; now they are, and nothing moves. On the same questions, provinces' gap on redistribution held at 0.19 to 0.26 of the disagreement inside a province from 2004 to 2019, and on the environment 0.12 to 0.20. Schools swing more (0.06 to 0.25) and the provinces' order isn't stable from one study to the next, which fits schools being the area with no clear winner. The 2008 carbon-tax question puts provinces at 0.18 against 2019's 0.24, in nearly the same order (correlation of provincial averages 0.90).

Only two inputs depend on these numbers. With 2004's figure for schools, disagreement inside a province can fall from the model's 0.25-0.30 to about 0.10; with 2008's for climate, the gap between provinces narrows slightly. Rerun on the same sampled worlds with both ranges widened, schools still pick equalization (no clear winner, 10% of the gain) and climate still a carbon price (99%). The environment is unaffected: even its largest dated gap leaves disagreement inside provinces above the model's ceiling.

Abroad

The same model on the United States

Three Canadian reforms are a small sample. The strongest test available is another federation: feed the model that country's measurements and ask whether its verdicts match how that country divides powers. Every measured input was rebuilt for the 50 states: state and local spending by function (Census Bureau, 2017-2022), market-income inequality (OECD), how strongly state spending rises with state income (BEA), CO2 per person (EIA). Mobility was refitted so the model reproduces US migration between states, about twice Canada's rate. Everything unmeasured keeps its Canadian range.

Two of five match. Redistribution comes out national, as it largely is in the US (Social Security, SNAP, the earned income tax credit). The environment comes out national too: the model prefers one standard, with a floor states may exceed close behind (it wins 29% of worlds), and the US uses federal minimum standards that states implement and may exceed.

Three don't, and the reasons differ. For climate the model says a national carbon price in every sampled world, as it does for Canada; the US has none, and the politics analysis above suggests why a visible price is hard to pass. For schools and transit the model favours equalization, because US state spending rises steeply with state income (an elasticity of 0.7 to 1.2 against Canada's 0.05 to 0.4, where equalization already exists). With US data, school equalization keeps 36% of the attainable gain against 10% for Canada: the model's case for it is stronger where it is missing. The US pays for transit and highways with matching grants, a rule the model says does slightly worse than leaving states alone.

What this test can and can't show. It is one country and five areas, so two matches out of five is weak evidence either way. The mismatches are where the model says the US arrangement leaves gains on the table, not proof the model is wrong; the model scores welfare, not what Congress passes. It still has 16 regions rather than 50 states, and it keeps Canada's cost-of-funds ratio, city map and unmeasured ranges.

Losers

Who loses under each rule

A rule can win on totals and still not last if its losses fall on a few places. For each recommended rule, 40 sampled worlds (3 seeds each) were run under the rule and under provinces deciding, and the change in welfare measured for the people who started in each of the 16 regions.

With real provinces: each region's own measured tastes, emissions and income

Each cell: the group's typical change as a multiple of the average gain (1.0 = gains like the average Canadian), and in brackets the share of sampled worlds in which it is worse off. Regions follow the model's 16: 4 Quebec, 6 Ontario, 2 British Columbia, 2 Alberta, 1 Manitoba and Saskatchewan, 1 Atlantic. Grouping hides Saskatchewan inside Manitoba.

Named, the losers are mostly Alberta. With every region set to its real province, a national environmental standard leaves Alberta with a tenth of the average gain and worse off in 46% of worlds; Manitoba and Saskatchewan in 23%. Alberta also gains least from national redistribution. On real welfare a carbon price leaves every group better off, Alberta included (about three quarters of the average gain). As voters see it, every group opposes a carbon price; a quantity standard is opposed mainly by Alberta (in 88% of worlds) and Manitoba and Saskatchewan (54%), and still passes.

The verdicts barely change. Giving regions their real provinces' values instead of random ones keeps the same kind of rule for the environment, redistribution and climate. Schools switch to a small matching grant and transit to a small national floor, both near-ties with no clear winner either way.

Equalization is the rule with concentrated losers. Under full equalization for schools (the best fixed rule there, though no clear winner), about a third of regions are worse off, and the hardest-hit loses over three times what the average person gains. That matches the politics: in 2021 Albertans voted 61.7% to remove equalization from the Constitution (a non-binding referendum).

The carbon price has almost no losers in the model. Even the two regions with the highest abatement cost, standing in for Alberta and Saskatchewan, gain about four-fifths of the average gain, because they share in everyone else's cuts. In the model, the reason the consumer charge didn't last is not lost welfare. See the backtest above.

A national environmental standard leaves few losers, and those are the regions that want less of it. Redistribution leaves about one region in eight worse off, by less than the average gain. Transit's best fixed rule, equalization, gains almost nothing on average and leaves over a third of regions worse off; named, Alberta loses in 56% of worlds and Manitoba and Saskatchewan in 48%. With no clear winner and those losers, there is no case for moving transit from the provinces.

Politics

Which rules would pass a vote

The model scores rules by real welfare; voters judge them by what they see. Two additions, applied after each simulation: under a carbon price households see the charge on every tonne they still emit but, as surveys found in Canada, little of the rebate; and a rule must win the regions holding a majority of people (or two thirds, standing in for the amending formula). Costs of quantity standards are buried in prices, so only a price has a visible payment.

A carbon price passes in every sampled world on real welfare, and in none once payments are visible. The charge households pay is about 19 times the real gain, so most regions would see a net gain only if voters noticed about 94% of the rebate (87% to 98% across worlds). That doesn't depend on the assumed awareness range: it would take near-complete awareness. The best rule that does pass is a quantity floor, which keeps about half the gain. Canada's 2025 change, ending the consumer charge but keeping industrial pricing and regulation, is the model's best passable rule.

Elsewhere politics costs little where the case is clear. National standards for the environment and redistribution pass in most worlds. Schools and transit, with no clear winner, are where votes bind: under a two-thirds rule nothing passes in about a quarter of worlds for schools (the best passable rule is usually a small matching grant) and about three in ten for transit. Since the gains there are small, so is what's lost.

Compared

Against published work

Each finding next to the closest published evidence. None of these authors has reviewed this project; full citations are in the repository's COMPARISONS.md.

No published study was found that scores Canada's division of powers across several policy areas with one calibrated model. The closest in spirit is Alesina and Spolaore's The Size of Nations (2003), the same trade-off between scale and differing preferences applied to borders rather than to who decides.

§ 9

What the model can't tell you

Sampling ranges fixes uncertainty about the numbers. It can't fix the model's structure. These would each move the answer, and none of them is in the simulation:

  • Data age. The data describe long-run structure (city sizes, the urban-rural split, which provinces emit more) from 2016 to 2024, and survey opinion from 2019. Structure changes slowly, and a reform to who decides would last decades, so a pre-pandemic year is arguably a better guide than 2021. Opinion and prices move faster: carbon-tax support has likely shifted since 2019. The model is not a forecast; where a verdict hinges on a fast-moving number, the threshold maps in § 6 show what value would flip it.
  • Politics. Governments here are honest median voters. Capture by industry, lobbying, and the gap between what voters want and what they get at each level are all absent.
  • Learning. Regions can't copy each other's successful experiments. That's one of the strongest real arguments for decentralisation, so the model probably understates it.
  • Uniformity. National policy has to be the same everywhere. Real national programs tailor somewhat, which narrows the case for regional control.
  • Who pays. Poorer regions now find local money dearer, and equalization is one of the rules tested. But equalization here tops up one service at a time; Canada's is unconditional and covers everything at once.
  • Scale. Sixteen regions on a grid is not ten provinces of very different sizes.

What two automated reviews challenged

The study's claims were run twice through ai-auditor, which extracts assumptions, searches for counter-evidence and proposes alternative answers. It is one model reviewing another, so its points are challenges, not verified findings, and studies it cites are checked against their sources before this page uses them. The second review gave the recommendations a confidence of 0.15 out of 1.

Fixed:

  • The verdicts restated guessed ranges. Replaced by Canadian measurements wherever any exist (§ 3); where none exist, verdicts are shown as thresholds (§ 6).
  • The cost-of-funds ranges excluded the cited evidence. A real error; corrected and re-run (see Corrections in § 7).
  • Equalization was never tested, though it is how Canada actually handles provinces' different ability to pay. Now one of the six rules.
  • The welfare measure was untested. Redistribution is now mapped across a range of weights on inequality (§ 6).
  • Overclaims. The page called the services-migration test a match (it isn't), presented an argument about the redistribution failure as if shown, and gave schools a verdict that is really a close call. All three are corrected.

Still open:

  • Transit and cities: addressed. Every census metropolitan area is now in the model ("One province, many cities"), and "cities decide" is a rule. Cities win only when almost no commuting crosses the metro boundary, and national wins only if one national system saves about 10% or more, which published cost studies suggest it wouldn't. Still missing: a Canadian estimate of how transit costs change with network size, and commuting between neighbouring metro areas (Toronto, Hamilton and Oshawa are separate areas in the census but share a commuter rail network).
  • Carbon pricing: now modelled as a separate climate area with prices, price floors and provincial differences in abatement costs (§ 4). Canada set its consumer carbon charge to zero on April 1, 2025; industrial pricing for large emitters continues under a national benchmark. Still missing: revenue use, trade exposure, and emissions leaking abroad.
  • Schools and language: partly addressed. Regions now have Canada's official-language profile, and a national standard costs minority-language residents unless it accommodates them (§ 6). Education is provincial under section 93 of the Constitution Act, 1867, which also protects denominational schools in some provinces (Quebec removed its guarantee in 1997); section 23 of the Charter protects minority-language schooling. A welfare total can't weigh those protections, which is the point of having them.
  • Provinces want different amounts of redistribution, and Quebec's residents move least: 0.35% a year leave for another province, against 0.55% to 1.99% elsewhere. Together these could favour a national floor with provincial top-ups.
  • Constitutional jurisdiction and Quebec's place in the federation are not welfare questions. A simulation can inform one consideration among several; it can't settle what Canada should do.

Read the results as a map of which features of a policy area push it up or down a level. They are not a verdict on any particular country.

§ 10

Run it yourself

Plain Python, standard library only. The optimised arrangement is saved as a scopelang program, so you can edit a law and re-score it.

python3 -m scopelang examples/min_wage.jur --tree
python3 -m tiebout study          # one hand-picked world, phase maps
python3 -m tiebout uncertainty    # sampled ranges, robust policies
python3 -m tiebout evaluate results/robust_arrangement.jur
